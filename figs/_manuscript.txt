Abstract Does perceptual grouping reflect the recovery of external structure, or does the visual system actively impose its own organizational constraints when the environment provides none? Here we show that it is the latter, governed by a capacity-limited, satisficing geometric prior. We tested this across four behavioral experiments (N = 280, &gt;23,000 trials) and neural network, using point arrays that provably lacked any generative cluster structure. Humans imposed reliable, shared partitions on these random arrays: grouping granularity emerged as a stable individual trait, response times scaled sublinearly with array size, and confidence tracked the internal separability of participants' own solutions rather than consensus, all of which serve as​ hallmarks of satisficing, not optimization . A neural network trained on human partitions learned this prior, reproducing four geometric statistics never included in its loss and reaching 85% of the human consensus ceiling. Crucially, a dose–response analysis formally demonstrates that no single capacity constraint can simultaneously recover both human signatures; the relationship is a monotone trade-off that definitively rules out a fixed resource cap. These findings recast perceptual grouping as an active, resource-bounded construction of order from randomness, challenging the view of vision as passive recovery and offering a quantitative target for human-aligned machine perception. Introduction When sensory input is ambiguous, underdetermined, or entirely random, observers spontaneously parse continuous fields into discrete units, partitioning noise into signal-bearing groups with striking cross-individual consistency 1–8 . This drive operates at the earliest stages of perception, shaping what we see before conscious interpretation 9–11 . The random-array paradigm of Marupudi &amp; Varma 12 quantifies this phenomenon with remarkable precision: under full-view, freehand grouping of provably structureless arrays, both the number of groups and their size scale invariantly with array size. Why does the system impose this particular amount and kind of structure?​ Standard accounts in machine learning and cognitive modeling frame perceptual grouping as an optimization problem, specifically one of​ maximizing statistical separability or minimizing description length 13–18 . These models assume unbounded computational capacity: more data or computation simply yields better solutions. Yet human groupings are systematically less geometrically compact than algorithmic optima, vary strongly with set size, and are constrained by the number of items actively maintained 19–25 . The discrepancy is not a minor quantitative misfit; rather, it signals​ a missing constraint that truncates the search space before optimality is reached. Existing frameworks have not specified how such a constraint operates algorithmically, nor why it manifests as graded rather than binary. We propose that perceptual structuring under ambiguity is governed by a capacity-limited satisficing process.​ Two pressures shape this construction : covering the input with enough groups to keep any one manageable, and preserving geometric coherence within each group (compactness, convexity, spatial continuity) , and because they pull against each other, the limit balancing them need not be a fixed ceiling. This framing makes a falsifiable prediction: if the constraint were a hard capacity cap, some single setting should reproduce human behavior; if instead it is graded, matching one signature (the rate at which group number grows with array size) should necessarily distort another (partition fidelity), with no single setting recovering both. The gradedness of the boundary, on this account, is not a vague "soft limit" but a measurable monotone trade-off with no sweet spot. Testing this hypothesis requires a grouping phenomenon that is already robust and precisely quantifiable, so that any inferred mechanism targets a real regularity rather than a task-specific quirk.​ Existing studies provide exactly that.​ But these​ behavioral characterizations leave open the mechanistic question: what computes the prior, and how does resource limitation enter? Prior modeling efforts have treated capacity as a tunable hyperparameter or assumed it away entirely, approaches that cannot distinguish a fixed cap from a graded trade-off. What is needed is a method that can reverse-engineer the human prior from behavior and then subject candidate mechanisms to a dose–response analysis, asking not "what parameter fits best?" but can any single constraint recover the full profile? We combined four behavioral experiments (N = 280, &gt;23,000 trials) with permutation-invariant neural networks to meet this need.​ Around random-array paradigm we built a 2 × 2 design: a memory-based occlusion condition (a moving aperture with dwell-time reveal) tests whether structure imposition survives serial glimpses ; an anchor-based Voronoi response format reproduces the same partition through a categorically different motor action, testing invariance to output modality. Crossed, the two factors separate perceptual access, motor response, and individual differences; we recorded response times and confidence to test for satisficing signatures. Computationally, we trained networks on human partitions to reverse-engineer the geometric prior, then introduced an explicit per-group capacity penalty , not to find an optimal parameter but to test whether any single-resource constraint can recover the full behavioral profile at once. All arrays contained no generative cluster structure, isolating the pure act of structure imposition. Our findings recast perceptual grouping as construction rather than recovery. Humans imposed reliable, shared partitions of the random arrays ( internally reproducible and more consistent than any standard clustering algorithm ) . Moreover , we found that the two invariant slopes persist across both visibility and response format but are systematically reallocated: anchor placers absorbed array growth into existing groups, whereas loop drawers opened new ones ; so the direction of each invariant is a general property of the system while its magnitude is a tunable parameter of the grouping policy. Grouping granularity was a stable individual trait, and response times scaled sublinearly with array size , in both the drawing and the deliberation components, consistent with bounded rather than exhaustive processing. Human partitions were also systematically less separable than the algorithms’ own optima, and confidence tracked the internal separability of a participant’s own partition rather than its agreement with the group consensus: signatures of satisficing rather than optimization. A network trained only on human partitions learned the human geometric prior ( reproducing silhouette, convex-hull ratio, and the cluster-size slope without those statistics ever entering its loss, and reaching 85% of a human consensus ceiling ) . Architectural enhancements could not close this gap , adding an explicit capacity penalty recovered the qualitative dissociation, but no setting simultaneously matched the human slope and fidelity. The relationship was a monotone trade-off with no sweet spot , and this indicates that the human constraint is not a fixed cap and cannot be rescued by tuning a single resource parameter. When environmental structure is absent, then, human perception does not stay structure-neutral: it actively constructs organized representations under a graded, satisficing resource constraint. Result Human grouping imposes stable, reliable structure with format-dependent reallocation Throughout, agreement between two partitions of the same array is quantified by the adjusted Rand index (ARI) 26 unless the Fowlkes–Mallows index (FM) is required for comparability. Although the arrays contain no generative clusters, participants partitioned them reliably (Fig. 1). The two presentations of a stimulus yielded highly similar partitions in every experiment (internal reliability, participant-level mean FM = .72 [95% CI .70–.74], .67 [.65–.69], .71 [.69–.73], .68 [.66–.69] for Experiments 1–4). Different participants converged on similar partitions of the same array (inter-participant agreement, mean FM = .62 [.60–.63], .60 [.59–.61], .62 [.61–.63], .61 [.60–.61]). The invariant slopes replicate in all four conditions but are reallocated by response format . A s array size increased, participants increased both the number of clusters and the size of clusters—neither quantity was conserved (Fig. 2 a,b ) in every experiment. Mixed-effects estimates of the number-of-clusters slope were +.096, +.073, +.031, +.031 (Experiments 1–4; all p &lt; 10⁻²⁰), and of the cluster-size slope +.144, +.142, +.260, +.239 (all p &lt; 10⁻⁴⁷). The direction of both invariants is thus robust to masking and to response format. The magnitudes, however, are systematically reshaped by format. In a pooled model (number of points crossed with visibility and with format; random intercepts for participant and stimulus), the anchor (Voronoi) format lowered the number-of-clusters slope (raw interaction β = −.046, standardized β = −.165, t = −22.2, p = 5.8 × 10⁻¹⁰⁸) and raised the cluster-size slope ( β = +.101, standardized β = +.201, t = 40.7, p &lt; 10⁻³⁰⁰) relative to lasso, whereas masking had only a small effect on the number-of-clusters slope ( β = −.013, standardized β = −.045, t = −5.8, p = 6.6 × 10⁻⁹) and none on the cluster-size slope ( β = −.004, standardized β = −.009, t = −1.7, p = .082). The fixed effects explained 14% (number of clusters) and 21% (cluster size) of the variance (marginal R²), rising to 65% and 46% with the participant/stimulus random effects (conditional R²). In short, the decision to "open a new cluster" versus "grow existing clusters" is preserved in direction but redistributed by how the response is made. Because visibility and format are between-participant here, these are between-participant contrasts, interpreted conservatively (Fig. 2c). G rouping granularity is a stable individual trait. Using the twice-presented stimuli, each participant's style parameters were highly reliable across presentations. For the median number of clusters, test–retest r = .90 (95% CI [.84, .94]) and .86 [.78, .91] in Experiments 1 and 3, with the same conclusion under an absolute-agreement intraclass correlation, ICC(A,1) = .87 and .85; for median cluster size, r = .82 [.72, .89] and .92 [.88, .95], ICC(A,1) = .81 and .92. Grouping granularity is therefore a stable disposition of the individual (Fig. 2d) rather than a trial-level property . These results identify the between-participant variance in the ceiling analysis (below) as structured signal, not noise. Behavioral signatures of satisficing: bounded effort, subjective confidence, and non-maximal separability In the full-visibility experiments, response time scaled strongly sublinearly with the number of points (cf. 27 ): the per-participant log–log exponent was b = 0.46 [.40, .52] for lasso (Experiment 1) and b = 0.19 [.11, .27] for anchors (Experiment 3), both far below 1 (lasso t ₆₆ = −18.1, d = −2.21, BF ₁₀ = 1.3 × 10²⁴; anchor t = −21.3, d = −2.60, BF ₁₀ = 9.3 × 10²⁷), and the two formats differed ( t = −5.9, p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁷). This sublinearity was not an artifact of motor execution: decomposing each lasso response into drawing (pen-down) and deliberation (non-drawing) time, both components scaled sublinearly (drawing b = 0.51 [.45, .57], BF ₁₀ = 3.4 × 10²¹; deliberation b = 0.37 [.29, .44], BF ₁₀ = 2.1 × 10²³), and deliberation departed from linearity significantly more than drawing (paired, same participants: Δ b = −.15, t ₆₆ = −5.9, p = 1.4 × 10⁻⁷, dz = −0.72, BF ₁₀ = 1.0 × 10⁵). Moreover, the near-drawing-free anchor format was the more sublinear of the two ( b = 0.19 vs 0.46), the opposite of a motor-time account. Bounded effort is therefore a property of the grouping computation, not of the response device (Fig. 3 a,b ). Confidence does not track consensus. In the funnel experiments (per-trial confidence rating), confidence did not track a trial's agreement with the leave-one-out group consensus ( l asso p = .85, anchor p = .32, p ooled p = .33). Confidence slopes favored the null ( BF ₀₁ = 3.7 lasso, 3.5 anchor, 2.4 pooled). The same analysis had ample sensitivity to detect an effect, because confidence strongly tracked the internal separability (silhouette) of a participant's own partition ( β = .35, BF ₁₀ = 8 × 10¹⁴ lasso; β = .07, BF ₁₀ = 291 anchor; pooled BF ₁₀ = 2 × 10¹⁵). Participants' metacognition thus reflected how clean their own solution looked, not whether it matched other people (Fig. 3d). A learned geometric prior under a graded capacity limit A neural model shows the grouping prior is learned, not a generic clustering rule (lesion). We compared Model-H against two standard, off-the-shelf clustering objectives: a Gaussian mixture model (GMM), which fits a fixed number of multivariate-normal components by maximum likelihood via the EM algorithm and is a canonical model-based clustering baseline 28,29 , and DBSCAN, a density-based algorithm that groups points by local density and tolerates arbitrary cluster shapes and noise 30 . A permutation-invariant assignment network trained on human partitions (Model-H) predicted held-out human partitions at ARI = .446/.445/.435 across three seeds (mean .442). Matched networks differing only in their training target performed far worse on the identical human test set (GMM target .218; DBSCAN target .310; shuffled-label target .155). In a crossed mixed model (ARI ~ target + participant + stimulus random effects), Model-H exceeded every lesion by a wide, stimulus-robust margin (vs GMM target Δ = .224 [.212, .235], dz = 1.01; vs DBSCAN target Δ = .132 [.121, .143], dz = 0.44; vs shuffled Δ = .287 [.276, .298], dz = 1.07; all z &gt; 27, p &lt; 10⁻¹⁶; Model-H higher on 8/8, 7/8 and 8/8 test stimuli respectively). Model-H also exceeded the agreement that the GMM algorithm's own partitions reach with humans (.242; Δ = .201, dz = 0.72, higher on 7/8 stimuli); its advantage over DBSCAN's self-agreement (.357) was reliable at the trial level ( dz = 0.27, p &lt; 10⁻⁶¹) but not consistent across the 8 test stimuli (4/8, p = .46), so we do not claim it over-tops DBSCAN. The lesioned models also failed to reproduce human summary statistics that were never in the loss: the shuffle model collapsed to ~2 clusters and the GMM-trained model over-segmented (mean k = 6.7), whereas Model-H matched the human cluster count and separability (mean k = 3.3 vs 3.9; silhouette .37 vs .36). The human grouping prior is thus a specific, learnable regularity not recovered by any standard clustering objective (Fig. 4a–c). Performance is calibrated against a human consensus ceiling. Because the task is subjective, we benchmarked the model against humans rather than a correct answer, on the identical test set. A single participant predicted another single participant at ARI = .393 (within format; stimulus-clustered 95% CI [.34, .47]), whereas the leave-one-out consensus of the other participants reached ARI = .517 [.45, .60]—the human ceiling. Model-H (.442 [.37, .54]) sits between the two, at 85% of the ceiling. Although these marginal intervals overlap (only 8 stimuli are stimulus-disjoint in the test set), the within-stimulus comparison is unambiguous: Model-H fell below the consensus ceiling on all 8 stimuli (Δ = −.075, dz = −0.36, p = 4 × 10⁻⁸⁷) and exceeded the single-participant baseline on all 8 (Δ = +.049, dz = +0.37, p = 7 × 10⁻⁷⁵). The residual gap to the ceiling coincides with genuine between-person variation (Section 3). Agreement fell monotonically as two partitions differed in cluster count (mean ARI = .45, .43, .36, .32, .25, .23 for | Δk | = 0…5), for humans and model alike (Fig. 4d). Human partitions do not maximize cluster separability (satisficing). Across the same arrays, human partitions were markedly less separable than those of DBSCAN, an algorithm that uses a comparable number of clusters: median silhouette .362 for humans versus .465 for DBSCAN (crossed mixed model, human − DBSCAN Δ = −.139 [−.146, −.132], z = 42, p &lt; 10⁻¹⁶, dz = −.57; human lower on 7/8 stimuli), even though DBSCAN's cluster count (mean k = 3.35) is close to the human value (3.87). GMM achieved a similar-to-slightly-higher silhouette (.412) only by over-segmenting (k = 5.36); the human–GMM silhouette difference was negligible and not robust (mixed-model Δ = +.019, per-stimulus 6/8, p = .69), so we do not rest any claim on it. Humans thus neither maximized separability ( at matched granularity DBSCAN separates the same points far better ) nor minimized cluster count (their k lies between the two algorithms). Together with the bounded effort of Section 4, this indicates that human visual grouping satisfices a good-enough partition rather than optimizing the separability objective that defines "good" clustering (Fig. 3c). The cluster-count invariant reflects a graded capacity limit, not architecture. Model-H reproduced human silhouette, convex-hull ratio, cluster count, and the cluster-size slope, but not the cluster-count slope. Across a 3 × 2 architecture ablation (mean/max, distance-pooling, or attention interaction; with or without an explicit point count), the recovered number-of-clusters slope stayed near zero for all 18 models (range .002–.033; one-sample vs the human value .061: t ₁₇ = −22.1, p = 6 × 10⁻¹⁴), even a s richer interaction raised overall fit (ARI .42 → .48). A layer-wise probe showed human-like grouping structure already in the early hidden blocks (ARI ≈ .50 – .54, peaking at .536 for attention), well above a raw-coordinate baseline ( .20) and the network's own output layer ( .44). The missing slope is therefore not a limit of representation or architecture scale (Fig. 5 a,b ). Adding a per-group occupancy penalty (which discourages large groups) generated a positive cluster-count slope and simultaneously reduced the cluster-size slope , confirming a capacity-like pressure as the right mechanism family. But no single penalty strength satisfies both slopes at once. Without the penalty the network reproduces the human cluster-size slope (.28 vs .26) but not the cluster-count slope (.02 vs .06); across a densely sampled dose–response (10 λ × 5 seeds), every penalty weight that raises the cluster-count slope toward the human value drives the cluster-size slope down to a plateau of .15–.21, well below the human .26. At the interpolated weight λ* = .030 that reproduces the human cluster-count slope, the cluster-size slope falls short of the human value by Δ = .085 (95% CI [.082, .089]; one-sided t = 71.7; BF ₁₀ = 1.9 × 10⁴). Human grouping therefore lies outside the achievable frontier of any fixed-capacity model (Fig. 5c); the model's partitions nonetheless remain structurally close to the human consensus across this range (fidelity cost ΔARI = .012, 95% CI [−.002, .026], BF ₁₀ = 1.7). The cluster-count invariant is thus set by a graded, not a fixed, capacity. Discussion When faced with provably structureless input, human perception does not discover nonexistent structure; it actively imposes a partition 31,32 . Critically, this imposition is not governed by fixed geometric laws; it reflects a learnable prior, one that can be shaped by experience (on the unsupervised discovery of latent structure and learned perceptual representations, see 33–36 ). We know this because when we trained networks on different targets (human partitions vs. off‑the‑shelf clustering objectives vs. shuffled labels), only the network trained on human behavior reproduced human‑like partitions. The human prior is therefore not a byproduct of any generic clustering algorithm; it is a distinct, learnable inductive bias. Perceptual inference has long been cast as approximation to a normative optimum, a Bayesian ideal observer, a good clustering solution 16 . Our data point the other way. Human partitions are systematically less separable than standard clustering solutions (silhouette is lower for humans (0.362) than for GMM (0.412) or DBSCAN (0.465)). Humans do not maximize cluster quality; they follow a leaner geometric criterion. This carries a theoretical implication: perception, at its entry level, satisfices rather than optimizes. It extends Simon's bounded rationality from deliberate choice to perceptual inference itself 37–42 . We cannot rule out that humans optimize some unmeasured objective. Our claim is therefore bounded. They do not optimize the separability criteria that define "good clustering" in the standard framework. We do not assert a metaphysical claim that the brain is a satisficer; we assert a relative, empirically grounded one. There is no single correct partition of a random array 43 . Human‑human agreement is inherently moderate (median ARI ≈ 0.39). Yet group‑level consensus is stable (consensus ceiling ≈ 0.511), and individuals are systematically biased toward it. "Subjective" does not mean "arbitrary", each individual is executing a different instantiation of the same prior. This has a methodological implication: evaluating human partitions against a ground truth is a category error (in the spirit of rational analysis 13 ). The appropriate yardstick is the structure of consensus plus the structure of individual differences. The human invariant is already established: the number of groups increases with set size (slope_k &gt; 0), and cluster size does not remain constant. Our explanatory contribution is to localize the mechanism: no architectural change reproduced this pattern, but a penalty on large per-group occupancy did produce the correct qualitative dissociation , identifying a capacity-like pressure as the relevant mechanism family. A caveat: this constraint is not a fixed threshold. Across the full dose–response series even the weakest penalty overshoots (slopes 2–3 times human, while fidelity drops), and no setting matches both slope and separability. The human limit is therefore graded (soft), not a fixed ceiling. How the system expresses this graded constraint while preserving fidelity remains an open question , one we frame as a new problem, not a failure. We used only 56 random point arrays from a single paradigm. w hether it generalizes to natural scenes or real‑world objects remains an open empirical question. But the mechanistic properties we identify ( learnable, satisficing, capacity‑governed ) do not depend on the specific geometry of the dots. The deliberate poverty of the stimulus is a design choice, not a limitation: it strips away semantic cues and pre‑existing structure, allowing us to ask cleanly what humans do when faced with pure randomness. S econd ly, the satisficing claim cannot completely rule out optimization of an unmeasured objective. For theories of perception, this work provides a concrete, falsifiable counterexample to the view that perception approximates a single normative optimum (cf. 44 ). For cognitive science, it suggests that the granularity of perceptual organization is shaped by a resource limit, not solely by stimulus statistics 38 . For methodology, the approach offers a general toolkit for reverse‑engineering subjective priors 45–49 in any domain where human judgment is the reference. Together, these findings redefine perceptual grouping not as a recovery of latent structure, but as an active, resource‑bounded construction of order from randomness. Conclusions We set out to ask what the visual system does when confronted with input that contains no structure to recover. Across four experiments and more than 23,000 trials, the answer is consistent: it neither waits for structure to emerge nor searches for the statistically optimal partition ; it imposes a good-enough one. Human groupings of random arrays are reliable and shared across individuals, stable across radically different response formats and under memory-based occlusion, and yet systematically less separable than the partitions standard clustering algorithms produce , marking the signature of satisficing rather than optimization. By training neural networks directly on human behavior and then lesioning the training target, we showed that this regularity is a specific, learnable prior rather than a by-product of any generic clustering objective, and that such a network reaches 85% of the human consensus ceiling while reproducing statistics it was never trained on. The one property no architecture could capture : the linear growth of group count with set size was moved toward the human value only by an explicit per-group capacity constraint, which nonetheless overshot that value, indicating that the underlying limit is graded rather than fixed. Perceptual grouping, on this account, is neither the recovery of latent structure nor the optimization of a normative criterion, but a capacity-limited, satisficing construction of order , one whose content is learned and whose boundary is soft. Characterizing how a bounded system expresses that graded constraint while preserving fidelity is, we suggest, the natural next problem. No single fixed-capacity model jointly satisfies both growth signatures. M ethod General method Participants and design. Four independent samples were recruited online through the Naodao platform, each corresponding to one cell of a 2 × 2 between‑participant design crossing stimulus visibility (full vs. funnel) and response format (lasso vs. anchor). Experiment 1 (full‑visibility, lasso; N = 67, 38 female, mean age = 24.0, SD = 3.6), Experiment 2 (funnel, lasso; N = 71, 39 female, mean age = 23.7, SD = 3.9), Experiment 3 (full‑visibility, anchor; N = 67, 36 female, mean age = 24.0, SD = 4.3), and Experiment 4 (funnel, anchor; N = 73, 31 female, mean age = 25.5, SD = 4.7). No participants were excluded based on our preregistered inclusion policy. All participants gave informed consent according to procedures approved by the Research Ethics Board of Sun Yat-sen University. The target sample size was informed by a previously reported effect of cluster structure on cluster area (Cohen's *d* = 0.80) 12 . Whereas that study drew on a combined in‑person and online sample, the present study was conducted entirely online; our smallest achieved sample (N = 67) provided adequate power to detect an effect of this magnitude (α = .05, 1 − β = .80). Stimuli. Stimuli were provably unstructured point arrays. Each array consisted of 10–40 white dots (radius = 5 px) on an 800 × 500 px black canvas, generated by uniform random sampling with a hard‑core constraint (minimum inter‑dot distance = 12 px; 7 px margin). Arrays were retained by rejection sampling on the standardized Clark–Evans nearest‑neighbour statistic (vacuumed *z*‑score), selecting clustered arrays (std‑vz ∈ [−2.05, −1.95]) and dispersed arrays (std‑vz ∈ [0.95, 1.05]); the two structure levels therefore differ only in aggregation statistics, not in the presence of true clusters. Fifty‑six base stimuli (7 point‑counts × 4 clustered + 4 dispersed exemplars) were used, together with a small number of practice stimuli generated by the same procedure. The stimulus set was fixed across participants and experiments, so that every participant saw identical arrays. For computing silhouette scores, Euclidean distances between points were calculated on the raw pixel coordinates (800 × 500 canvas) without normalization. To enable stimulus‑matched comparison with Marupudi &amp; Varma 12 , we reused their original stimulus set; the 56 arrays were identical to those used in that study. \ Procedure (common elements). All experiments were self‑contained JavaScript applications built with jsPsych (v6.1.0) and administered online in the participant’s desktop web browser using mouse input. A minimum‑window‑size check enforced adequate display dimensions before the task began. After consent and demographics, participants read on‑screen instructions emphasizing that dots should be grouped based on how close they look, without mentioning a target number of groups. They then completed 3 practice trials with feedback (repeatable) before the formal session. On each trial, participants grouped the dots into disjoint subsets, with an on‑screen counter showing the number of dots currently assigned. A trial advanced once 100 % of the dots had been assigned; a single‑group response triggered a warning and required the trial to be redone. Trial order was randomized. Full instructions are provided in the Supplementary Information. Response‑format specifics. Lasso (Experiments 1–2). Participants drew closed freehand loops around each group with the left mouse button; a loop was automatically closed upon mouse‑button release. Right‑clicking removed the most recently drawn loop. A dot was assigned to the first loop that enclosed it. Raw loop‑edge coordinates, together with mousedown and mouseup timestamps for each stroke, were logged, allowing separation of drawing time (sum of pen‑down intervals) and deliberation time (trial time minus drawing time). Anchor (Experiments 3–4). Participants placed anchor points on the canvas by left‑clicking; each dot was assigned to its nearest anchor, partitioning the array into a Voronoi tessellation whose cells were rendered in real time. Anchors could be added, dragged, or removed (right‑click) freely. Because every point is automatically assigned to the nearest anchor, no point can remain unassigned once at least one anchor is placed; thus the trial‑completion criterion (100 % dots assigned) was naturally satisfied. Mirrored repetitions. For 25 % of the stimuli, a mirrored variant was pre‑generated by reflecting the array through the canvas centre (*x* → 800 − *x*, *y* → 500 − *y*), a rigid transformation that preserves cluster structure while changing absolute positions. Each variant was assigned a unique identifier and pre‑assigned to experimental blocks, making the reflection status of every presentation fixed at design time. Experiment‑specific variations Experiment 1 (full‑visibility, lasso). All dots were visible simultaneously. The 56 stimuli were each presented twice, yielding 112 formal trials. The session lasted approximately 60 min. Experiment 2 (funnel, lasso). Stimulus visibility was restricted: the canvas was initially blank, and dots were revealed only within a circular aperture (radius = 240 px) that followed the cursor; a dot became visible after the aperture had dwelled over it for 1,000 ms. A bottom status bar displayed the number of dots currently assigned. Participants explored the array through the moving aperture before and while drawing loops; already‑enclosed dots remained visible in blue. The experiment used 28 unique stimuli, divided into 4 blocks of 14 trials each, with stimuli drawn randomly without replacement per block—some stimuli therefore appeared in multiple blocks, providing partial repetition as an internal‑reliability check (56 trials total). After each trial, participants rated their confidence on a 7‑point scale (1 = not confident at all, 7 = very confident). Occasional probe (attention‑check) trials displayed four highly separated clusters; failure to segment them correctly prompted a retry. Probe‑trial data were excluded from all analyses. Experiment 3 (full‑visibility, anchor). Identical to Experiment 1 in stimuli, visibility, and repetition structure (112 trials, 25 % reflected), but used the anchor response format described above. Experiment 4 (funnel, anchor). Combined the funnel visibility of Experiment 2 with the anchor response format of Experiment 3. The array was revealed through the moving aperture; each participant completed 56 trials (28 stimuli once, 14 twice), with per‑trial confidence ratings and detection trials as in Experiment 2. Behavior Analysis Each response was reduced to a partition of the array's points (which dot belongs to which group), from which all dependent measures were computed with a common pipeline. Following Marupudi &amp; Varma 12 , we computed: internal reliability and inter-participant agreement (Fowlkes–Mallows index over point pairs); the invariance of the number of clusters and cluster size with respect to number of points; cluster-quality indices (silhouette, Calinski–Harabasz, Davies–Bouldin); and the five cluster attributes. Point-level partition measures were analyzed with linear mixed-effects models, with number of points × cluster structure as fixed effects and random intercepts for participants and stimuli; model comparisons used likelihood-ratio tests, and fixed-effect Wald χ² statistics are reported alongside coefficients. A small number of degenerate trials in which all points were placed in a single group were removed prior to analysis. Effect sizes, equivalence, and Bayes factors. Descriptive comparisons are reported with an effect size and an uncertainty interval alongside the test statistic. Reliabilities (FM) are summarized at the participant level with 95% bootstrap confidence inte rvals (5,000 resamples over participants); where we add a one-sample t test against their published value and a two-one-sided-tests equivalence test with a ±.05 margin. For the invariant-slope moderations we report standardized coefficients (from models refit on z-scored predictor and outcome) and the variance explained by the fixed effects alone and with the random effects (Nakagawa marginal and conditional R²). Grouping-granularity retest reliability is reported both as a Pearson correlation and as an absolute-agreement intraclass correlation, ICC(A,1), from the two-way presentation × participant variance components. Response-time scaling exponents b (per-participant log–log slopes) are tested against 1 with a one-sample t test, Cohen's d, and a JZS Bayes factor, and the drawing and deliberation exponents are compared within participant with a paired test. Null and equivalence claims are quantified with JZS Bayes factors (Cauchy prior, scale r = .707) 50 , obtained by numerical integration; BF₀₁ &gt; 1 indicates evidence for the null. Unless otherwise noted, every response ( drawn loops or placed anchors) was first reduced to a partition of the array's points (which dot belongs to which group), and all downstream measures were computed from this common representation. This reduction makes the four experiments directly comparable despite their different motor demands. Behavioral analyses were implemented in R . Reducing responses to partitions. In the lasso experiments (1–2), a point's group was the loop enclosing it, taken in drawing order; the ~1.4% of points enclosed by more than one loop were assigned to the earliest-drawn loop. In the anchor experiments, a point's group was its nearest placed anchor, with empty cells discarded. Practice trials, attention-check (“detection”) trials, and the rare degenerate trials in which a single loop enclosed every point were excluded. Because reflected repetitions preserve the array's point ordering, the two presentations of a stimulus were compared point-for-point without spatial matching. For each partition we computed the measures: the number of clusters and cluster size (numerosity); three cluster-quality indices—silhouette, Calinski–Harabasz, and Davies–Bouldin 14,18,51 —each hand-implemented and cross-validated against R to a tolerance of 10⁻⁶; and five cluster attributes (continuous visual properties known to influence quantity judgments 52 )—numerosity, convex-hull area (Shoelace formula over the monotone-chain hull), density, linearity (the R² of an ordinary least-squares fit within a cluster), and convex-hull ratio (proportion of a cluster's points lying on its hull). Because each stimulus was presented twice (Experiments 1 and 3) or a subset twice (Experiments 2 and 4), and because reflected repetitions preserve the point ordering, partition similarity across the two presentations of a stimulus was quantified with the Fowlkes–Mallows (FM) index over point pairs 53 , yielding an internal-reliability measure. the same index applied across participants for a given stimulus yielded an inter-participant agreement measure. Partition-level measures were analyzed with linear mixed-effects models: number of points × cluster structure as fixed effects, with random intercepts for participants and for stimuli. Fixed effects are reported as coefficients with t statistics (Satterthwaite) and as Wald χ² = t² with the corresponding p value; nested-model comparisons used likelihood-ratio tests. Following the original study's trial-level rule, the small number of degenerate trials in which all points fell into a single group was removed before analysis; no participants were excluded. The two invariant slopes—number of clusters versus number of points, and cluster size versus number of points—were estimated in every experiment and constitute the quantitative link between the behavioral and computational analyses (see below). Four analyses exploited the manipulations . To test whether the invariant slopes are specific to the drawing task or the full-visibility condition, we pooled all four experiments and fit a mixed-effects model with number of points crossed with visibility and with response format (plus cluster structure and the same random-intercept structure). Using the twice-presented stimuli of Experiments 1 and 3, we estimated each participant's style parameters (median number of clusters and median cluster size) separately on the first and second presentations and computed their test–retest correlation across participants. High retest correlation indicates that grouping granularity is a stable individual disposition rather than a trial-level property. In the full-visibility experiments, we estimated the exponent b of response time on number of points (log–log slope); b &lt; 1 indicates that effort scales sublinearly with array size. To confirm that this sublinearity reflects the grouping computation rather than motor execution, we decomposed each lasso trial's total time into drawing (pen-down) time and deliberation time, and estimated b separately for each component; the anchor format, whose responses are near-instant pointing clicks with negligible drawing, served as a converging low-motor control. Controlling for the number of clusters drawn and the number of points (with random intercepts for participant and stimulus), we further tested whether longer times were associated with more separable partitions. In the funnel experiments (which collected a 1–7 confidence rating per trial), we tested whether confidence tracked a trial's agreement with the group. Trial-level agreement was defined as the adjusted Rand index (ARI) between a participa nt's partition and a leave-one-out consensus partition of the same stimulus, and confidence was regressed on this agreement while controlling for n umber of points and partition silhouette. The absence of a consensus – confidence relationship is quantified with a Bayes factor rather than a non-significant p value: we fit per-participant regressions of confidence on standardized consensus agreement, own-partition silhouette, and number of poin ts, and computed a JZS Bayes factor on the participant-level agreement slopes , the same test applied to the silhouette slopes serves as a positive-control sensitivity check. Computational model Architecture and training. The assignment network used a model width of 128, four set-interaction blocks, and K_max = 20 output slots; the funnel variants added a bidirectional GRU (hidden size 128) over the aperture-reveal order. Networks were trained in PyTorch with AdamW (learning rate 6 × 10⁻⁴, weight decay 10⁻⁴), a one-cycle schedule, and bfloat16 mixed precision, for up to 200 epochs at a batch size of 256 with early stopping on held-out ARI (patience 25, at least 40 epochs); a small entropy term (weight 0.01) sharpened the soft assignments. Each network configuration was trained with three random seeds (the capacity dose-response used five), and results are reported across seeds. Training used RTX 5090 GPU s . We modeled the mapping from a point array to a soft partition. A permutation-equivariant assignment network embeds each point with a shared MLP 54,55 , optionally passes a bidirectional GRU over the scan/reveal order (used for the funnel experiments, which carry a genuine reveal sequence), applies several set-interaction blocks in which each point is updated from itself together with the array's global mean and max 56 , and outputs, per point, logits over Kₘₐₓ = 20 assignment slots followed by a softmax. The number of clusters is therefore emergent: the network may populate any number of slots, and how many it uses is determined by the objective rather than being specified (cf. 57 ). The training objective is a pairwise co-assignment loss: for each point pair, the model's predicted co-assignment probability Aᵢⱼ = Σ_k p_ik p_jk is compared by binary cross-entropy to the target indicator that points i and j share a group. This loss is invariant to cluster relabeling—necessary because human cluster labels (drawing order, anchor order) are arbitrary—and is isomorphic to the FM index. We deliberately did not apply positive-class reweighting to the pairwise loss: because merging clusters increases the number of within-group pairs, such reweighting rewards under-segmentation (empirically collapsing partitions to two clusters). A small entropy term encouraged near-hard assignments; regularizers that would write human biases directly into the loss were disabled (weight 0) and used only for the capacity analysis described below. Data were split by stimulus (base_uuid; fixed seed 42; 10% validation, 15% test), so that models generalize to unseen arrays rather than to unseen responses of seen arrays (cross-validation 58 ). Models were trained with AdamW and a one-cycle schedule under mixed precision, with checkpoint selection and early stopping on ARI (see Analytic choices). Lesion analysis. To ask whether the network learns a human grouping prior rather than a generic clustering rule, we trained matched networks that differed only in their target partition: Model-H was trained on human partitions; Model-R on partitions produc ed by Gaussian-mixture (BIC-selected K ∈ [1, 8]) clustering, by DBSCAN ( ε = twice the median nearest-neighbour distance, min_samples = 2), or on shuffled human labels (a random relabeling that preserves the number of clusters per trial but destroys its spa tial content). All models—regardless of training target—were evaluated on the identical held-out human partitions, so that the comparison is on a single scale. Beyond overall fit, we evaluated a set of statistics that are not present in the loss (number of clusters, silhouette, convex-hull ratio, and the two invariant slopes); a model that reproduces these while a lesioned counterpart does not is taken to have learned the human prior rather than the fit target. Human ceiling and satisficing benchmark. On the identical held-out set, we estimated a human consensus ceiling by comparing each participant's partition to a leave-one-out consensus of the remaining participants (co-association → average-linkage → cut at the median number of clusters), computed within response format; single-participant-to-participant agreement was also recorded to characterize the intrinsic subjectivity of the task. Separately, to test whether human grouping maximizes cluster quality, we compared the silhouette of human partitions to that of the GMM and DBSCAN partitions of the same arrays 30 . The coupling between agreement and granularity was characterized by binning held-out partition pairs by their absolute difference in cluster count (|Δk| = 0–5) and averaging ARI within each bin, for human–human and model–human pairs alike. Model evaluation statistics. All model comparisons are made on the identical stimulus-disjoint test split, at the level of the individual held-out trial (adjusted Rand index between the model's and that trial's human partition). Because the split contain s many participants but few distinct stimuli, we report each contrast three ways: a crossed linear mixed model (ARI ~ training target, with random intercepts for participant and stimulus) giving treatment-versus-Model-H contrasts; a stimulus-clustered bootstrap 95% confidence interval for each mean (resampling whole stimuli); and a within-stimulus paired test (mean ARI per stimulus), which is robust to trial-level non-independence. Model-H is compared this way to each lesioned target, to the two clustering algorithms' own agreement with humans, and to the single-participant and leave-one-out consensus ceilings. The satisficing separability comparison is analyzed identically, with a crossed mixed model of per-trial silhouette (silhouette ~ source [human, GMM, DBSCAN], random intercepts for trial and stimulus) and treatment-versus-human contrasts. Architecture ablation and capacity mechanism. To localize why the network reproduced most human statistics but not the cluster-count slope, we ran a 3 × 2 architectural ablation crossing the pairwise-interaction mechanism (global mean/max only; fixed-kernel distance pooling; distance-biased self-attention 56,59 ) with the explicit provision of the point count, and we probed each hidden layer by clustering its representations and comparing the recovered partition to the human one against a raw-coordinate baseline. Motivated by these results, we augmented the loss with a per-group capacity penalty, Σ_k ReLU(occ_k − c)² normalized by array size, where occ_k is the soft occupancy of slot k and c is a fixed target occupancy (set to 4); we then swept its weight over a dose–response series (three seeds per level) rather than tuning it to the human slope. This analysis is reported as a mechanism test, not a fit: we ask only whether a capacity constraint moves the cluster-count slope in the human direction. To test whether any single penalty strength can reproduce both empirical slopes at once, we then densely re-sampled the weight in the critical low range (λ = 0, .01, …, .08, .10; five seeds each; 50 networks) and, per seed, linearly interpolated the weight λ* at which the recovered cluster-count slope equals the human value. The pre-registered primary endpoint was the cluster-size-slope gap at λ* (human minus model), tested one-sided against zero and against a pre-registered equivalence margin of .05 (a minimal-effect test) with a JZS Bayes factor (scale r = .707); the change in agreement (ΔARI vs. the unpenalised network, margin .02) was a secondary endpoint. Hypotheses, endpoints, margins, and the decision rule were fixed before the re-sweep. The recovered ablation slopes were compared to the human value with a one-sample t test (exploratory); the capacity dose–response is the pre-registered confirmatory analysis.
